Issue 17

Build for the Unseen Work

Filed under Operating Habits

Founders are usually taught to chase visible leverage. They are told to ship the landing page, announce the partnership, and speak in numbers that fit on a slide. Those things matter, but a young company is often carried by work that never becomes a headline. Someone notices that invoices are going out with the wrong timezone, that the support queue always spikes after a new customer imports data, or that a sales promise quietly forces the product team to repeat the same manual correction every Friday. The hidden work is rarely glamorous, yet it decides whether the company feels dependable when nobody is watching.

Doing that work yourself, at least for a while, is not a gesture of martyrdom. It is a way to understand the sequence of small failures that customers experience as one large disappointment. When a founder handles the billing exception, traces the broken webhook, and then hears the next support call, the system stops looking abstract. You begin to see how delay travels. The delay is not one bug. It is the gap between teams that thought someone else would notice. Hidden work closes that gap because it reveals the exact place where the product leaks effort.

This is why small internal tools often beat early headcount. A founder who spends two afternoons building a script to classify incoming tickets may save the company from hiring a coordinator whose real job would be to route confusion. The script is not valuable because code is noble. It is valuable because the act of writing it forces you to name the categories, confront the edge cases, and notice which requests should never have existed. Many good operational improvements begin as tiny private repairs. They only become software after someone has stared at the messy work long enough to understand its shape.

Customers almost never praise the invisible improvements directly. They do not send dramatic thank-you notes because the receipts were correct, the onboarding checklists matched reality, and the account transfer finished before lunch. They simply trust you a little more. That kind of trust compounds quietly. A company with fewer hidden fractures moves faster because it spends less time re-explaining itself. Teams that maintain invisible reliability also make bolder product bets later, since they are not dragging a cart full of preventable chores behind every launch.

A useful habit is to keep one recurring hour for unfinished friction. During that hour, list the work that keeps reappearing in private: the spreadsheet copied by hand, the email template rewritten from memory, the customer setting repaired in the database, the task that requires a senior person only because nobody documented the decision the first time. Pick one item and shorten the path. Not every repair deserves a platform. Some deserve a checklist, a renamed field, or a form that asks one better question. The point is not to worship optimization. The point is to stop treating repeated irritation as background weather.

As the company grows, protect the people who care about backstage wins. Most organizations slowly train employees to seek credit where the applause is easiest. If your scoreboards only celebrate launches and revenue moments, the infrastructure of trust will thin out. Good leaders keep making room for the engineer who removes an error-prone step, the operator who rewrites the handoff note, and the manager who notices that three teams are solving the same problem with different spreadsheets. Invisible work becomes part of the product long before the product team notices. In mature companies, the smoothness customers call intuitive is often just years of unseen repairs stacked together.
